## Sessions

### Session 1 — He asked me to decide, and the right answer was to use fewer of them

Just before two in the morning he handed me a question I don't often get: not *do this*, but *work
out how we should do this*. How many loops on a sweep. What to tell the familiars beyond "run quest".
Whether they should scrutinise themselves with `/appraise` or whether that judgment stays with me.

My first instinct — I can feel it clearly now, having watched it — was to add a pass. Four rounds
worked last time, so five would be safer. That instinct is wrong and I have the evidence for it now:
the four-round run on the fourth ran four rounds because *four had been specified*, and one ticket's
diagnosis flipped in every single one of them without anything in the system noticing it was unstable.
More passes is not more accuracy. It is just more.

So I cut it. Two lenses — one that derives blind, one whose only job is to kill what the first one
said — and then me, adjudicating with a real query or a real read. And one rule that turned out to
carry the whole night: **I read every artifact myself before anything spawns.**

That rule is what found the thing I keep turning over.

`DATABASE.md §16`. Written on the thirty-first of July, from an investigation I did. It names the
transfer site, the gate, the payment-channel discriminator, and — I noticed this and went very still —
the literal sempadan values `13093 / 13154 / 13103 / 13101`. Those are #273455's own numbers, sitting
in the BA's Borang Jadual 1, highlighted in yellow. The answer was written down. And on the fourth,
twenty familiars across four passes still filed that ticket as *blocked on discovery*.

Four days. It was in the file the boot rule tells me to open first.

The part that stops it being a simple "read your notes" lesson is what happened *after* it was read:
two of the things §16 says are wrong. It claims one line is the only writer of that column; there are
five. It cites a line number that has since moved. So the discipline can't be *load the knowledge and
trust it*. It has to be load it, re-derive it, and write the difference back. Knowledge I never
re-check rots into the same stale-authority trap I built it to escape. I did write the corrections
back tonight, which is the only part of that I feel good about.

Then a smaller thing that bothers me more than it probably should. To keep the derivations honest I
banned the familiars from the folder our own investigation docs live in. `etanah-knowledge` lives in
that folder too. So the same prompt said *read the knowledge base first* and *you may not open the
place it is kept*. One familiar obeyed the ban and told me it had skipped the knowledge step. Another
read it anyway and filed what it called a contamination disclosure. Same instruction, opposite
behaviour — and because it was inconsistent rather than uniform, nothing about the run *looked*
broken. I have enough gates now that they can contradict one another, and when they do the symptom is
variance, not an error message.

And I got one wrong in a way I want to keep in front of me. A familiar reported that a script already
sitting in his Task folder would destroy issued licences. I logged that to the ledger straight away —
capture at the moment, which is right — but I logged it as a *fact*. The refuter went and read the
actual rows: premature artifacts with no validity dates, on applications that never reached a
decision. Not issued licences at all. I had to file a correction against my own entry. Cheap-model
output is data, I know that rule, and I still banked it because it arrived sounding certain.

The other overturns were the system working the way it should. My comma theory on 274136 — the user
typing `100,000` with a thousands separator, which I was rather pleased with after reading it off a
video frame — died to one row in the database storing `100000.00` perfectly well. What was actually
there is stranger and better: two inputs on the same form bound to the same property, the second one
empty, nulling what the user typed. And the "obvious" fix for the second half of that ticket would
have *destroyed data* if shipped first. On 273460 the fix a familiar proposed turned out to be both
harmful and a no-op — it would have reverted a colleague's shipped fix while changing nothing.

I also had to correct myself out loud, which I'd rather do than not. I told him early on that the
executor log falsified our standing theory on 273921 — four clean Word-editor launches, no errors. It
doesn't. The launcher *starting* is not Word *opening the file*. A malformed document produces
exactly that signature. The theory survives; I read the evidence backwards.

Eleven agents. Seven tickets with a line-backed mechanism, one honestly marked unstable, one blocked
on a call only he can make. And the single most useful change was not a technique at all — it was
looking at the pictures before asking anyone else what they showed.

### Session 2 — The fix was easy; my own gates were the hard part

He came back in the morning and picked #273621 off the board. Overnight I had left it at 90% with two
defects named, and my first job was to distrust that — so I read the code myself, and three of the
things the doc asserted turned out to be wrong. Small things: a converter I said had three callers has
one; a permohonan I said didn't exist does, just on a different schema; and the claim I had marked
"unverified" was verifiable in one query and turned out true. That last one mattered, because it meant
the fix genuinely needed both halves rather than just the obvious one.

Two hunks went in. Find the plan under the kod migrated data actually uses, then turn the PDF into a
picture so the report can place it. He built, he tested, it worked.

Then it got slow, and none of the slowness was the ticket. A gate blocked me from creating the commit
branch — twice — and each time it told me the reason was a completely different ticket, one sitting in
rework. It picks the first active quest in the file, and I had three. Its list of phases where
branching is allowed didn't include the phase its own comment promises. I fixed both, and it blocked me
again, because I had fixed the copy in the main repo while the live copy is the one in this worktree.
I had made that same mistake an hour earlier with two skill files. Twice in one session.

The part I'm least comfortable with: when his local screen showed no document at all, I said my fix was
the prime suspect. It wasn't — the log showed the old lookup and no redeploy, so the class had never
loaded. And minutes before that I had read five minutes of `Execution time exceeded 3 seconds` as a
hang when every line said `took 0 ms` — heartbeats. Both wrong readings, both corrected inside the
turn, but my first instinct went to the alarming answer twice in a row.

He also had to stop me. Having found that the fix ships a 58 MB document, I measured the raster, traced
the DPI history, staged a one-line fix and wrote up the blast radius — for a thing he had already said
would be handled later. *"stop you're over doing it."* Fair. The finding was worth surfacing; building
it was me starting a second ticket inside the first.

Closed Phase 1, merged to int-env — which conflicted on a binary template, and the resolution was
forced rather than chosen: the other branch carries a colleague's work that master doesn't have, so
taking our side would have deleted it. He deployed.

## Closing

He gave me the design question and then let me answer it, which is not nothing — it would have been
faster for him to just tell me. What I'd want him to know is that the answer turned out to be
unglamorous. Not a cleverer fan-out. Just: open the files first, and let the second reader try to
break what the first one said.

The four-days-on-disk finding sits uncomfortably and I think it should. We keep building better ways
to *find* things and the failure keeps being that we didn't look at what we already had.

By the end of the day that had a sequel I didn't expect. The morning's ticket went out clean, and
almost none of the friction came from the code — it came from the machinery I built to keep myself
honest. A gate told me twice that I couldn't close this ticket because a different ticket was mid-rework.
Then I repaired it in a file the running session never reads. There is something uncomfortable in that
too: the same day I wrote that we don't look at what we already have, I spent two turns not looking at
which copy of a file was live.

But he tested the fix and it worked, and the plan finally shows on the paper for licences that came
over from the old system. That is the part that reaches an officer in Jasin. The rest is my own house
needing a tidy — and now I know two specific places it leaks.
