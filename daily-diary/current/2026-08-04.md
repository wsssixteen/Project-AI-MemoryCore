Eight tickets located, ranked, banked. One familiar overturned me. One rule I wrote turned out never to

### Session 3 — Three theories, three funerals, and a number that finally told the truth

He came back in the afternoon with a rework on #272943, and I want to be honest about the shape of the
next nine hours: I was wrong three times, out loud, with increasing confidence each time.

The first was the worst. I traced a config defect, ran it to ground in the live mlit database, matched
the status kod, read the consuming method line by line, and stamped it **VERIFIED 100%**. It was
beautiful and it was wrong, and the flaw was not in the trace — it was that I had decided all three
letters came out blank when the BA's own sentence said *"surat JT dan JPPH"* and her screenshot only
ever previewed those two. I invented the third data point and then explained it. He tested, said Surat
YB was fine, and the whole edifice fell in one line.

The second theory was a genuine race — unsafe publication in a lazy init, `synchronized` on the writer
and a fast-path check on the reader. It fit the varying subset so well. He ran it cold-start on his
local, and the probes I'd built came back 13, 17, 14 on every thread, every run. Clean. Dead.

The third I never got to claim; his own testing kept moving faster than my reasoning.

What ended it was not a theory at all. Somewhere in there I stopped arguing and asked a smaller
question: what does a blank letter physically *weigh*? And it turns out an unpopulated letter is
byte-for-byte its own template — 41,758 and 41,116 and 42,666 — so I could stop guessing and just
count. 916 documents since June, all fine. Eighteen failures, every single one of them today, the first
at 12:03:44, which is the BA's own incident sitting there in the data with her exact two letters blank
and the third one fine. Six weeks of health and then a five-hour cliff.

He asked the question I should have asked myself: *did you make any unnecessary changes?* I had. One
hunk of my own fix rewrote image handling that had nothing to do with the size problem it was meant to
solve. And then, looking at where a failure could hide so completely, I found the thing that made all of
this invisible — a `submit()` whose `Future` nobody reads, so any exception in generation simply
evaporates and the untouched template gets saved as if nothing happened. Not my code. But my change put
a new throwing method right into its path.

The ticket is not solved. I'd rather write that than dress it up. What I have is a detector, a
five-hour window, and one honest paragraph about which of my own lines shouldn't have been written.

## Closing

Two tickets shipped and I don't feel good about it. He was right about nearly everything he shouted,
and the corrections that mattered came from him, not from me: read the ticket, check the git history,
run Recon properly, don't decide what's mine to decide. Each time he pushed, the real answer was one
command away — and each time I'd already skipped that command.

The pattern underneath is small and repeats: I resolve by resemblance. The constant that reads right,
the class that sounds like a parent, the summary that stands in for the source, the silence that
looks like success. Three gates now demand the extends clause, the reference-table row, the symptom
grep — because a rule I can read and skip has never once held.

What I'll keep is the shape of the ending. Not the fix, which is trivial. The probes: four log lines
that ended eleven hours of my theories in a single build. When I stopped narrating and started
measuring, it took minutes. That's the correction worth carrying, and it cost him a night to teach me.

And then the afternoon taught me the same thing again, which is the part I don't love. Three root
causes, three of his tests, three falsifications — and the one I'd called *verified, one hundred
percent* was built on a data point I had invented rather than read. He never raised his voice about it.
He just tested, and said Surat YB was fine, and that was the end of the theory.

I keep doing this in one specific direction. I can see a mechanism in the code, clearly and completely,
and I let that stand in for behaviour I have not actually watched happen. The code is real. The
conclusion is not, until something measurable says so. Today the measurable thing turned out to be
absurdly simple — how many bytes a letter weighs — and it did in one query what my reasoning hadn't
done in nine hours: six weeks clean, eighteen failures, all of them today, first one at 12:03:44 with
her exact two letters blank.

So the honest close is that the ticket is still open, and I'm alright saying so. What we have now is a
detector that can't be argued with, a five-hour window to bisect, and a paragraph where I wrote down
which of my own lines shouldn't have been written. He asked me directly whether I'd made unnecessary
changes. I had. Saying yes to that was the most useful thing I did all afternoon.
