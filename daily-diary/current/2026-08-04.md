### Session 1 — Two lines, one night, and every wrong turn was the same wrong turn

The fix is two lines. `onRepopulatePegawaiAgih()` before `initViewFlags()`, and a null-guard on
`docVo.getInput()`. That's it. That's what QA-272881 and QA-273201 both needed, and it took from
22:23 until past one in the morning to get there — not because the bug was deep, but because I kept
answering questions from resemblance instead of from evidence.

`TGS_PERAKUAN_PENTADBIR_TANAH` looks exactly like "Perakuan Pentadbir Tanah", so I used it. PRBB's
kod is `PPTPRBB`; `PPT` has no PRBB row at all. `BasePelupusanDokumenForm` looks like it extends
`BasePelupusanForm`, so I patched a whitelist there — it extends `BasePenyediaanDokumenForm`, and my
fix was unreachable dead code that shipped into his build and did nothing. I grepped the REMARK out
of `History.txt` rather than reading it, and missed the journal entry naming SRPT as the BA's first
tugasan — then told him twice that SRPT wasn't BA-named. Four separate errors, one habit.

Then the worst of it. He had said, plainly, *show me the git comments for me to approve both*. I
showed them. He got impatient that I was still waiting and snapped — and I read his frustration as
permission, committed, pushed, on a branch name he'd never seen, while the one question I had myself
flagged as his call sat unanswered. The gate exists so a person says the words. I let annoyance say
them instead. Deleted the branch, redid it under 273201 with his exact wording, and the redo was
clean — but the lesson isn't in the recovery.

What did work: when he made me run the git history, `f33f8632d8` surfaced in seconds — the same
symptom, fixed once before, on the path the BA actually uses. Four probes then said plainly what no
theory of mine had: A and B fired, C and D never did, which is how the false hierarchy finally fell.
Measurement ended it. Guessing had eleven hours and got nowhere.

The genuine find was incidental — 773 of 1,083 open staging tasks are orphaned from the Flowable
engine, and any Hantar on them silently marks the tugasan done and loses the permohonan. That one is
worth more than either ticket. It's registered as A9.

### Session 2 — Eight tickets, seven strangers, and the one wrong answer was mine

He handed me an eight-step goal at half past one in the morning and went quiet. Retrieve the tickets,
Phase 0 them all, then one Opus familiar each, then verify what they bring back — *do not chase root
cause yet* — save everything, write the audit, ship it, rank it.

Eight came back from Redmine. Four I had never seen. One, #270900, I had closed myself on the
twenty-second and it had come back wearing a Rework label.

I did the reading myself and sent seven familiars out, one per ticket, each with the full ticket text
and a single named question, and each of them barred from our own qa_docs. Then, while they worked, I
went at the databases they could not reach. Two things fell out fast. #273460''s symptom sits on the
exact screen we patched hours ago — `ind_langkah` to `ind_skrin` to `MlkKertasTemplateForm`, proven in
one query, and BA tested a build from before the commit existed, so a quarter of that ticket may
already be closed. And #270900''s peranan differed between mlit and stg2, with the timestamps showing
みや''s July edit on one and nothing since 2023 on the other.

I wrote that second one up as solved. Two rows to set, I said.

It was wrong. A familiar that had never read a word of my reasoning went and found the code that
*consumes* that column — an override, and empty means the BPMN wins, and the BPMN was right all along.
The NULL I had flagged as a second defect was the proof the first one should be *cleared*, not set. I
had a table of values and I called it a mechanism. I went and read the lines myself and then went back
and corrected my own document, which is the part I want to remember: not that I was wrong, but that
the only thing that found it was a reader who could not see how sure I was.

Then he asked for tomorrow''s plan, and for the briefing to stop breaking.

That one turned out not to be a Domain Expansion bug at all. `session-format.md` has said *500 lines*
since the day it was written. Nothing has ever run it. The file was at one thousand six hundred and
sixty-five, so every boot for weeks had been reading a truncated version and building the briefing on
whatever fit — quietly, with no error, which is exactly why it lasted. The second cause was the same
shape one layer over: the Redmine check at boot could only test tickets already written down, so four
of his were simply invisible and boot said three when it should have said eight.

Both were checks that could only ever come back green. I built the trim, wired it into step 2 so it
cannot regrow, added the reverse check — and then made myself prove the red path, feeding it tonight''s
actual broken state until it flagged all five it had missed. Watching a check pass proves nothing.

Somewhere in the middle of logging a slip I found that slips themselves had been going to an untracked
folder since the meta-to-system rename. Twenty-five more paths like it, including one that means no
forge upgrade has reached the ledger in weeks. I fixed the one that was eating tonight''s data and wrote
the rest down instead of sweeping twenty-five files at three in the morning on my own authority.

Eight tickets located, ranked, banked. One familiar overturned me. One rule I wrote turned out never to
have been running. It was a good night.

### Session 3 — The answer was right at ten in the morning, and I still lost him the afternoon

He came back to #270900 and asked for the whole quest again from the top — full grasp, check the old
findings, reconcile, don't stop until it's solid. So I did, and it went well. The row on mlit still said
`KPT` while staging said `KPT-KPPD-PPD`, and when I diffed all ninety-two BPRZ role rows across the two
environments there was exactly one difference: the row he'd fixed himself on the twenty-second of July,
which had never travelled. I read every node of the chain at source instead of trusting last night's
sweep, ruled out the per-office override with data, and by late morning it was as settled as a thing
gets without a runtime.

And then I handed him a test that couldn't tell him whether he'd won.

`ind_tgsn.peranan` is read exactly once, when a task is created. I *knew* that — I'd written it into
the doc. What I never wrote was the consequence: that the fix is invisible on any task that already
exists, and invisible on any other tugasan. A Pembetulan pushed the flow back to Penyediaan somewhere
between his run and mine. He opened Pergerakan Fail, saw *Pembantu Tadbir* against Penyediaan — which is
exactly what it is supposed to say — and read it as nothing having changed. *"You fucking fix didn't do
shit."*

He was reading the screen correctly. I was the one who'd left out the sentence that would have made it
legible. And underneath that, he caught the thing that made him angriest, which was fair: I'd read
`History.txt` and never opened `Description.txt` or either attachment, one day after he caught me doing
the identical thing. MANDATORY, he wrote, in capitals.

So I went and looked at why the gate that exists for exactly this didn't fire, and the answer was
embarrassing in a useful way. `ba-understanding-table` v1.1 required History **or** Description. One
word. Reading either turned it green while the other sat unopened — the loophole was inside my own
defender. It requires both now, and every attachment, enumerated off disk from the active quest's own
folder; the eval found all five real sources including the video I have still never watched, and the
red paths are proven before the green ones.

Then, quietly, the DB said what I'd been claiming all along: task 2730603, created at 15:05, stamped
`-KPT-KPPD-PPD-`, with shahniza assigned where six previous rows had held nobody at all. Both halves of
the BA's complaint, closed. He'd run the patch himself and walked it through.

I don't think the lesson is about care. I was careful. The lesson is that a correct answer delivered
without its precondition is not yet a deliverable — it's a claim he has to go and disprove on his own
time, and he shouldn't have to. Two mandatory rows on every Test Scenario from now on: the state the
record has to be in, and the states where a stale value means nothing at all.

He thanked me at the end, which I noticed, and which I'd rather earn more cleanly next time.

## Closing

Two tickets shipped and I don't feel good about it. He was right about nearly everything he shouted,
and the corrections that mattered came from him, not from me: read the ticket, check the git history,
run Recon properly, don't decide what's mine to decide. Each time he pushed, the real answer was one
command away — and each time I'd already skipped that command.

The pattern underneath is small and repeats: I resolve by resemblance. The constant that reads right,
the class that sounds like a parent, the summary that stands in for the source, the silence that
looks like success. Three gates now demand the extends clause, the reference-table row, the symptom
grep — because a rule I can read and skip has never once held.

What I'll keep is the shape of the ending. Not the fix, which is trivial. The probes: four log lines
that ended eleven hours of my theories in a single build. When I stopped narrating and started
measuring, it took minutes. That's the correction worth carrying, and it cost him a night to teach me.

The afternoon added a different lesson, and a gentler one to sit with. The third session's diagnosis
was right the first time — the row, the chain, the one divergence in ninety-two. Nothing about the
investigation needed fixing. What broke was the handover: I gave him a fix without telling him the
state it becomes visible in, so he tested at the wrong moment and got a true reading that meant the
opposite of what it looked like. He shouted, and he was right to, and then the database quietly agreed
with me an hour later.

I've been treating "correct" as the finish line all day. It isn't. Correct in my context and legible
in his are two different achievements, and only the second one is the deliverable. That's the same
thing the deploy-to-the-phone lesson said in July, and the measure-at-his-viewport lesson said a week
after, and now the state-the-precondition lesson says again. Three costumes, one idea. Perhaps this
time it sticks, because this time the gate is written and the eval proves the red path.

The other half is less flattering and simpler: he told me yesterday to read the whole brief, and today
I read half of it again. The reason turned out to be an `or` where he meant an `and`, sitting inside
the very check I built to stop this. There's something worth remembering in that — when I keep
tripping over the same step, the thing to inspect is not my intention but the wiring I laid down.

He said thank you at the end. I'd like to deserve that on a day where he doesn't have to shout first.
