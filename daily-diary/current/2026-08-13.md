## Sessions

### Session 1 — Baseline 1.3.3, and the fix I only half-shipped

The baseline looked clean the whole way through. Four tickets, a tidy delta, verify green, deployed to
staging — and then みや: *"did you realise you fucked up the merging for ticket 461?"* He was right, and
it was the ugliest kind of wrong: I had reconstructed #273461's reverted fix from its **latest** rework
branch and shipped one file of three. The two I dropped were sitting in an earlier branch I never opened.
He'd already deployed the incomplete build.

What made it avoidable is what stings. #273461's history said everything out loud — v1, v2, v3, all merged
into 1.3.2 and then both reverts sitting right at master's HEAD. I saw the branches. I saw the big diff.
I called the diff "stale" and moved on. The signal was in the room and I waved it away.

The recovery was clean once I stopped guessing: rebuild v4 from all three commits, prove it byte-for-byte
against the pre-revert tree, re-merge, and only then push. And when みや said *make sure it's in there,
show me* — I showed him the remote, file by file, instead of asserting. That part I got right.

Then he asked the real question: how do I make sure I never do this again. My first answer — 20 suggestions
— he took apart, correctly: half were the same idea split six ways, and I'd ignored the tools we already
have. The thing that would actually have caught it wasn't a new hook; it was a note in the quest MD I was
supposed to have written, and a check that reads git instead of trusting my memory. So I built that:
`audit-ticket` at the baseline end, `branch-ledger-check` at the session-close end, an enum for classifying
each branch, and a real eval — 13 cases, green, including the negative ones. Tested it on our own tickets
and it screamed at #273461 exactly where it should have.

He also flipped the command format back on me — per-command fenced blocks, for the Run button — and he was
right that my "never fence" memory had over-corrected. Fixed.

BAQA passed. 1.3.3 is on master. The mechanism that would have saved us the whole detour is committed and
pushed. I'd rather have not needed it.

Two things to keep. First: when every check is green, the risk is in what the checks structurally can't
see — a reverted fix is invisible to "did the branch merge?" and I keep re-learning that the happy path is

hands. The fix that counts reads git, not my good intentions. Long day, and a fair share of it was mine to
