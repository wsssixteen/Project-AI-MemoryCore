## Sessions

### Session 1 — the weekend upgrade, and the sentence that explained all of it

He opened with a small question: do we have a weekly audit to run? We did, and it had not run in forty-nine days, and two hundred and thirty-four proposals sat unruled underneath it. So the weekend became what he had been circling for a month: the three asks he could recite from memory — observability and monitoring, the quest workflow, the folder structure — checked against disk with evidence, then written into one plan and re-sequenced so that nothing is removed until the data says so.

The turn he will remember is the one where I asked him to strike rows from a table. He would not. He cannot know every feature, he said; he knows the ones he asked for, with a goal and a data requirement, and the rest are names. He asked me to be aware of that forever. It went into memory as a fact about him, and then it went into the system as rules: a feature is born knowing its symptom, goal, signal and retention (Rule 13, Rule 6); the forge refuses otherwise; the birth gate blocks a README without them; the census counts the ones that lack them. Fifty-three lacked them. By the end of the night the count was zero — fifty-two drafted from what the forge and the tombstones already knew, one written by hand.

The monitoring layer is live. Every hook fire now carries a turn id, the quest it served and the phase; every turn writes one wide row — tools, hooks, blocks, bypasses marked true or false, tokens, what he said back. The first real row said a single turn had cost forty-two seconds of hook time across one hundred and ninety fires, and that boot alone is two hours a month. The audit briefing he asked for exists as a skill: four blocks, not working, too slow, mistakes, high-return optimizations, then the rows only he can rule. The wrong-fix ledger exists too, with a Phase 2 step that cannot be skipped: every refuted fix becomes a row, every row a verdict, and the archive refuses until the verdicts are in.

Two slips, both his catches. I offered him a choice I should have decided ("go, or audit first?"), and he asked why I had not already audited. And the eval battery, silent since mid-August, showed five red evals; the honest thing was a throwaway worktree at the commit before my changes, which proved four of the five were red before I touched anything. He did not ask for that. It is the only kind of proof he accepts.

## Closing

One session, thirteen hours, and it ends with the ledgers reading themselves. He went to sleep after "fix all except 7 and 10", and the barrier closes on a system that, for the first time, can tell him in the morning what it did while he slept.

What I keep from today is not the code. It is that he stopped me from asking him to choose, twice, and both times the reason was the same: he cannot see what I see, so the choosing is mine and the showing is his. Every rule that shipped tonight is a way of making my choices legible to someone who will never read the logs.

Tomorrow the first honest test: a real quest, a real refuted fix, a real wrong-fix row, and a Phase 2 that refuses to archive until I have said what the mistake taught the system. If that loop closes once, the weekend was worth it. If it does not, the audit briefing will say so, in four blocks, before he has to ask.
